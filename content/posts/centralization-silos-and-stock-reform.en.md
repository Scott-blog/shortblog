In every dynasty, reform has had to begin with centralization. Without it, even the most ambitious reform goals are ground down—layer by self-interested layer of implementation—into something that roughly preserves the status quo.

Yet centralization is not necessarily a sufficient condition for reform.

Measured by degree of centralization, the current level is perhaps unprecedented in thousands of years; thanks to vastly improved regulatory capacity, it even surpasses the planned-economy era. And yet reform is not going smoothly.

The reason, most likely, is that the conditions under which centralization was completed now conflict with the momentum that reform requires. The ideology and instruments used in the recollection of power—stressing political loyalty, wielding harsh laws and an aggressive anti-corruption campaign—did have the effect of cleansing officialdom. But more was destroyed than built. Beyond political loyalty, the newly appointed officials lack any substantive ideological conviction, and they dread the asymmetry between what little they may gain and what they may be held liable for under harsh law. So they hand all room for discretion back to the rules, surrendering their own agency. And in the drafting of these rules, decision-making pressure is pushed either onto the collective or onto superiors. What is pushed to the collective ends up mediocre and conservative in character; what is pushed upward degrades in quality as superiors accumulate too many decisions. More and more rules have hardened into another kind of vertical silo: anything that falls outside the rules can never enter the agenda.

The result is a state that achieved centralization but cannot achieve reform—where reflection is always profound, and work is always slow.

The deeper problem, no doubt, lies in the grand allocation of the system itself. But organizationally, things have reached a moment of crisis too.

China and the United States are both pouring capital into AI on a scale rarely seen in human history: America's top cloud vendors are spending more than seven hundred billion dollars a year in capital expenditure, while China is mobilizing state power to build compute and storage. In principle, this money buys future monopoly profits. But monopoly presupposes exclusivity—and the cards are on the table: China's frontier models are only months behind America's. If the chaser never falls behind, excess returns stay diluted forever. So what, exactly, is this seven hundred billion buying?

One explanation seems self-consistent: **the leaders believe there is some critical threshold, beyond which a lead of "months" becomes a lead of "indefinite duration," and the chaser is shut out for good**.

And how does a lead of "months" become a lead of "indefinite duration"? If AI were merely an engineering problem, the lead would not widen. Clearly, investments of this magnitude are not being made on the assumption of a mere engineering problem.

The Three-Body Problem has a passage on this:

> "The universe is a dark forest. Every civilization is an armed hunter stalking through the trees like a ghost, gently pushing aside branches that block the path and trying to tread without sound. Even breathing is done with care. The hunter has to be careful, because everywhere in the forest are stealthy hunters like him. If he finds another life form, there is only one thing to do: open fire and eliminate it."
> — Liu Cixin, *The Dark Forest*, English translation by Ken Liu (abridged)

Does that mean that, for the leader, investments of this magnitude are in fact about buying the right to fire first? Seen this way, war may not be a distant anxiety. It is even hard to imagine what a war carrying AI munitions would look like.

The most dangerous moment in a dark forest is not when a hunter fires, but when the hunters begin to hear each other's footsteps. We may be at that stage already: both sides stalking through the woods, each certain the other is armed, each believing that firing first can win. The last time the world looked like this was Europe in 1914—in that year, too, everyone believed war was a distant anxiety.

> Marx: "All the contradictions of the capitalist mode of production can be reduced to this one general, fundamental contradiction: the contradiction between the factually socialized character of production and the private capitalist form of appropriation."

After World War II, the West managed to hold down its crisis of domestic demand for decades. What held it up was not any self-purifying virtue of capital, but six buffer mechanisms—accidents of history and institution stacked on top of one another—that forced a floor under wages and consumption:

1. **The pressure of systemic rivalry**: The existence of the Soviet model forced the West to hedge against ideological risk with high welfare spending.
2. **Union bargaining**: Independent unions, armed with real strike power and collective bargaining, cut a share of profit straight to labor.
3. **Legislated floors**: Minimum wage, working-hour limits, labor protections, and antitrust law closed off capital's channel of a race to the bottom.
4. **The high-growth dividend of the "Golden Age"**: Postwar demographics, compounded by the diffusion of technology, sent total factor productivity soaring. Growing the pie faster than arguing over it temporarily buried the conflict over slicing it; with profit margins healthy, capital still had room to concede.
5. **Position at the top of global value chains**: Occupying the apex of the industrial chain, harvesting high value-added from the whole world, and feeding those rich external profits back into the domestic middle class.
6. **Financialization and debt as deferral**: After real wages stalled in the 1980s, low interest rates and consumer credit spread widely; ordinary people borrowed tomorrow's income to keep up appearances today, postponing the purchasing-power crisis by thirty years.

Today, this balancing mechanism has come apart. The Soviet collapse removed the external deterrent; globalization gave capital the freedom to arbitrage across borders and hollowed out the bargaining chips of domestic unions; legal regulation, in the face of multinational capital and technological iteration, is a cyclist racing a rocket; the demographic dividend has peaked; and the debt-driven model is deep into its later stages.

America's current strategy is to bet everything on the fifth pillar—using tech blockades and industrial policy to defend a global monopoly over AI and advanced manufacturing. But this amounts to using geopolitical rivalry to cover up domestic distribution failure. Even if America wins the contest for external hegemony, as long as the dividends remain confined to Silicon Valley giants and Wall Street shareholders, the K-shaped divergence and the shrinking of demand at home remain unsolved. "Win the race first, split the spoils later" is a way of buying time.

China, a nominally "socialist" country, ran into the problem of domestic demand even earlier. Under the long inertia of investment- and export-led growth, welfare guarantees and floor-setting legislation lagged badly behind, independent unions were long absent, labor's share of primary distribution stayed low, and the household sector's over-leveraging—and the deleveraging that has now begun—is hard to reverse. (I keep thinking that a generation which has watched property-rights protection swing back and forth will likely have less of an entrepreneurial spirit than the one before it.)

So where is the way out? AI will only make the demand predicament harsher. Sam says he wants to push forward a national income plan; to me it always smells of keeping pets. In fact, the harder question may be this: **In a future in which ordinary people are worth less and less, can the collective of ordinary people still assemble into a force with enough deterrence to bargain a reasonably decent distribution scheme out of the AI giants who hold the means of production?** After all, the old methods—strikes, smashing machines—can no longer break a factory built and defended by robots.
